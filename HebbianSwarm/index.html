Emergent Heterogeneous Swarm Control Through Hebbian Learning

Tugay Alperen Karagüzel1 Andres Garcia Rincon1 A.E. Eiben1 Dario Floreano2 Eliseo Ferrante1,3
Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam1 · École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne2 · NYU Abu Dhabi3
Paper Code arXiv

Abstract

In this paper, we introduce Hebbian learning as a novel method for swarm robotics, enabling the automatic emergence of heterogeneity. Hebbian learning presents a biologically inspired form of neural adaptation that solely relies on local information. By doing so, we resolve several major challenges for learning heterogeneous control: 1) Hebbian learning removes the complexity of attributing emergent phenomena to single agents via local updates, thus circumventing the micro-macro problem; 2) uniform Hebbian learning rules across all swarm members limit the number of parameters needed, mitigating the curse of dimensionality with scaling swarm sizes; and 3) evolving Hebbian learning rules based on swarm-level behaviour minimises the need for extensive prior knowledge typically required for optimising heterogeneous swarms. This work demonstrates that with Hebbian learning heterogeneity indeed naturally emerges, resulting in swarm-level behavioural switching and significantly improving the capability of the swarm.

Heterogeneous swarms


From fish evading predators to ants that organise to building elaborate tunnel structures, in nature collective behaviour in groups of animals have demonstrated the effectiveness of group coordination. The complex dynamics of collective behaviour is hard to dissect and often cannot be understood when analysing the behaviour of a single individual, as group-level properties emerge through interaction with other group members. This obfuscation is exacerbated when we consider swarms with different behaviours, reflected in heterogeneous properties and specialisation within the swarm. Designing robot controllers on an individual level such that a group of robots obtains emergent properties at the group level, lies at the heart of swarm robotics. Three main challenges arise in the design of heterogeneous swarm controllers:

  1. Micro-macro problem: Emergent capabilities are measured at the swarm-level but adaptation are made on an individual control-level, thus attributing individual contributions to the whole is ambiguous.
  2. Curse of dimensionality: The number of possible interactions between swarm-members explode when increasing the size of the swarm. Optimising larger swarms become exponentially hard.
  3. Prior knowledge: Pre-defining heterogeneous composition —for example number of sub-tasks and types of specialisation— reduces the flexibility of the swarm to scale or adapt during deployment, making the system more brittle.

Continuous Hebbian adaptation


We propose Hebbian learning as an elegant solution to these problems. In contrast to conventional neural network optimisation —where a training phase results in a network with a set of fixed weights during deployment— Hebbian continuously adapt dynamic weights during deployment through the use of local learning rules (i.e. \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\)). In swarms, these local \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\) result in the emergence of heterogeneity, as each individual experiences a unique sequence of sensory inputs.

Each robot applies the same Hebbian adaptation rules. Distributing uniform \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\) decouples the optimisation search space from the swarm size, making it independent of the number of robots.

During deployment, adaptations are governed by the \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\), that update the weights inside the network. Only local information is used, namely, correlations between input activity and output activity of the neurons directly associated with each weight.

The same \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\) result in unique adaptations as each individual experiences a unique sequence of network activations. Among swarm members network weights diverge during deployment, leading to the emergence of heterogeneity.

Results


Emergent heterogeneous swarm control through hebbian learning elegantly solves the aforementioned challenges: 1) Local information governs the updates of the network weights during deployment, which alleviates the micro-macro problem; 2) Uniform \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\) constrains number of design variables, thus circumventing the curse of dimensionality; 3) Emergence of heterogeneity is a consequence of the online adaptation mechanism, thereby removing the choice on heterogeneous structure based on prior knowledge.

We optimise our \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\) for a source-localisation task, by using an evolutionary algorithm (CMAES see Top Figure). Hebbian adaptation leads to behavioural switching

During deployment, we notice distinct behavioural transitions in the swarm. Initially (circular spiral trajectories), the individuals in the swarm scatter to find the direction (i.e. the gradient) of the light source as a collective. Once the gradient is found (straight trajectory lines), the swarm collectively aligns and moves towards the source, i.e. an online behavioural switch emerges by adaptation. In a final stage (rotation around the centre), the behaviour of the swarm scatters the individual around the centre, circling back when individuals are leaving the group. This switching is not pre-programmed but rather emerges through local adaptation, showcasing real-time adaptation and decision-making.

Persistent weight dynamics under perturbation

We found that the switching dynamics are persistent during deployment. The first half of this perturbation experiment is indistinguishable from the previous static trial, with the scattered spiralling behaviour for gradient estimation and collective switching towards the centre of the arena. After we perturb the position of the light we can see the same sequence of behaviours reoccurring, indicating that the online behavioural switching is a persistent capability of the local adaptations in the swarm. This is further evidenced by the average autocorrelation of the network weights that increase in the Perturbation experiment, indicating higher similarity of weights after switching the light.

Increased robustness in sim-to-real transfer

Finally, we test our \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\) on a real robot swarm. Due to limited resources, we scale down our experiment in terms of size (\(8\times8\) metre arena) and swarm size (10 robots), while keeping all other conditions equal. We present the difference between the average simulator performance (striped bars, \(N=60\)) and average real swarm performance (solid, \(N=3\)) in the bar graph, as a metric for the Reality Gap. The percentage performance drop (\(\Delta\)) is the Lowest for our Hebbian controllers with respect to two state-of-the-art methods that evolve neural networks (again we refer to the Paper for more details).


Closing remarks and additional links


Hebbian learning enables adaptive, heterogeneous behaviors in robot swarms—without the need for central coordination, role definitions, or hand-crafted designs. The key takeaway is the emergence of diversity and seemingly collective switching as a consequence of purely local interaction through \(\textrm{Hebbian-Rules}\). Consequently, Hebbian learning is a robust approach that effectively works when transferring controllers from sim-to-real. This approach opens new avenues in designing flexible, adaptive, and autonomous robotic collectives.
